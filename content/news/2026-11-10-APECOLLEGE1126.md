WASHINGTON (AP) — The midterm elections already<a href="https://apnews.com/article/election-takeaways-midterms-trump-democrats-takeover-republicans-1cb09c8674598632ec3e4307eda5fbb8"> upended national politics</a>. Due to the number of<a href="https://apnews.com/article/democratic-gains-battleground-states-midterms-elections-e3abf3135f626e37d8d2f57e5591ea6a"> state governments that have flipped</a> to Democratic control, they also could change the way the U.S. elects a president.

The sudden blue tint to so many more statehouses opens the door to enough states joining 18 other Democratic-run states in a deal that would effectively neuter<a href="https://apnews.com/article/electoral-college-election-explainer-trump-harris-ca14e0823c1e88aa5e5039645637a357"> the Electoral College</a>. It would remake the battle for the presidency into one for the most votes nationwide, rather than candidates concentrating on a half dozen or so swing states.

The states that have joined what is known as the<a href="https://www.nationalpopularvote.com/"> National Popular Vote Interstate Compact</a> account for 222 Electoral College votes. Once the number of states involved hits the 270<a href="https://www.congress.gov/crs-product/R48936"> needed to win</a> the presidency, the arrangement would effectively require a candidate to win the national popular vote to get an Electoral College majority.

It’s not clear that the strategy has support from all the newly elected lawmakers and governors in the key states, and it’s almost certain to draw legal challenges from conservative groups. If it does go forward, it could land before the conservative-majority Supreme Court.

Yet the sweep of the Democratic wins for governor and in state legislatures has the plan’s supporters optimistic. In Tuesday’s elections, Democrats won full control in six states representing 71 Electoral College votes: Arizona, Michigan, Minnesota, Nevada, Pennsylvania and Wisconsin. Minnesota, with its 10 electoral votes, had joined the compact when it was previously under full Democratic control in 2023.

## Some governors express support while others are noncommittal

“We can do away with it,” Wisconsin’s new<a href="https://apnews.com/article/wisconsin-governor-crowley-tiffany-ee1355fb426f242638e0fc4d1e5674b5"> Democratic Gov. David Crowley</a> said of the Electoral College, now that he has a legislature in his party’s hands for the first time in 16 years. “I am interested in having those conversations with the state Legislature.”

Over the weekend, Pennsylvania Gov.<a href="https://apnews.com/hub/josh-shapiro"> Josh Shapiro</a>, a likely 2028 contender for the Democratic presidential nomination, said he would like his newly Democratic legislature to take steps toward making the Electoral College obsolete.

“I think that the national popular vote makes sense. My views on that have not changed,” Shapiro said on a liberal podcast. “I’ll work with lawmakers to figure out what the right mechanism is for moving forward on that.”

If Pennsylvania and Wisconsin supported the compact, reaching the 270 threshold would require<a href="https://www.archives.gov/electoral-college/allocation"> Michigan and either Arizona or Nevada</a> to join. On Tuesday, the office of Michigan’s new Democratic governor,<a href="https://apnews.com/hub/jocelyn-benson"> Jocelyn Benson</a>, said it would not comment on the matter.

Not every Democratic governor is embracing the push yet. In a television interview on Monday, Arizona Gov.<a href="https://apnews.com/hub/katie-hobbs"> Katie Hobbs</a> declined to say whether she favored her state joining the compact. She said she’s focused on other issues, like the cost of living.

Nevada’s previous experience with the issue also is a cautionary tale for supporters.

In 2019, then-Democratic<a href="https://apnews.com/united-states-presidential-election-general-news-political-news-a654f94f03a5445999c6111d0e5eb873"> Gov. Steve Sisolak vetoed a measure</a> that would have committed Nevada to joining the compact after it was passed by Democratic majorities in both houses of the Legislature. Sisolak said at the time that the popular vote idea would diminish the influence of smaller-population states like Nevada in national elections.

Democrats passed another measure to join the compact in 2023, but couldn’t figure out a way around the state’s then-Republican governor. Tai Sims, a spokesman for Nevada’s incoming Democratic governor,<a href="https://apnews.com/article/nevada-governor-race-2026-lombardo-ford-07bca480a52cedc4148e0cd1f11e6248"> Aaron Ford</a>, said in a Tuesday statement, “There will be a time to talk about presidential elections, but right now Ford’s focus is lowering costs for working families.”

Republicans will use their diminished power to push back.

“Our Constitution gives states a voice in choosing the president for a reason,” Tyler August, the Republican leader in the Wisconsin Assembly, said in a statement Tuesday. “This would undermine that balance and disenfranchise Wisconsin voters by letting results in other states override their choice.”

Two presidents 16 years apart lost the popular vote

The idea of shifting to a national popular vote gained traction among Democrats after Republican George W. Bush won the 2000 presidential election by inching ahead of Democrat Al Gore in the Electoral College even while losing the popular vote. It became turbocharged after Republicans again won the White House<a href="https://apnews.com/article/e261a5597eaf442c8ad3f8f7ffa43f30"> that way in 2016</a> with Donald Trump’s victory over Democrat Hillary Clinton.

Under the National Popular Vote plan, the candidate who<a href="https://apnews.com/article/AP-explains-elections-popular-vote-743f5cb6c70fce9489c9926a907855eb"> wins the popular vote nationwide</a> would receive the Electoral College votes from the states that have joined the compact.

There’s nothing inherently partisan about the idea, said Patrick Rosenstiel, a Republican strategist in Minnesota who is a senior consultant to the popular vote movement. Occasionally GOP-majority legislative chambers, including the Oklahoma Senate, have passed proposals to join the compact, but the bills haven’t gone further.

He said his own presidential vote is essentially meaningless because Minnesota has reliably voted Democratic in presidential elections for decades, leaving him with no say in the outcome and steering<a href="https://apnews.com/article/electoral-college-2024-wisconsin-illinois-trump-harris-cdb4141205034c830924cb94457b7be1"> the vast majority of the candidates’ attention</a> elsewhere.

“I don’t believe I’ve ever been relevant in the outcome of a presidential election,” he said. “National popular vote forces campaigns to focus on every persuadable voter in every precinct across the nation.”

Rosenstiel said the national popular vote campaign has been revving up to push the issue when the newly Democratic state legislatures convene at the start of the year. He said the organization has already lined up legislative sponsors.

## The compact could create constitutional concerns

<a href="https://apnews.com/article/trump-2026-midterms-voters-young-voters-hispanic-voters-democrats-congress-ba1689d8402415d26ad32f4fa80ab2f9">Republicans no longer have the power</a> to stop the change in the Democratic-controlled states, but some conservative groups are prepared for a fight. The change “disenfranchises voters in member states if enacted, violates the United States Constitution, and undermines America’s Founders’ intent,” wrote analysts for the America First Policy Institute, a pro-Trump think tank, in September.

There also is no guarantee the proposals will pass. Derek Muller, a Notre Dame law professor who is skeptical of the idea, said some might have second thoughts now that it’s gone from a symbolic protest vote to a real possibility. He said there will be constitutional concerns, as well, that will likely lead to legal challenges.

The Constitution lets states run elections, and the Supreme Court in a case stemming from electors who sought to find a substitute for Trump in 2016 has<a href="https://apnews.com/article/district-of-columbia-ap-top-news-elections-courts-electoral-college-a398c3e7c8d2c4a1b9ad498ed5f86ed7"> ruled that states can require electors</a> to vote for the presidential candidate they’re pledged to.

Muller flagged potential legal hurdles to the popular vote plan. States have the power to run their elections but not set rules that will determine the outcome in other states’ contests. Additionally, each state ballot is different, and some would potentially have third-party candidates who wouldn’t appear elsewhere, leading to the dilution of a vote for a Democrat or Republican in one state compared to another.

That could violate the 14th Amendment’s requirement that everyone have “equal protection of the laws.” The U.S. Supreme Court also might decide that states can’t join a compact to change one of their core constitutional duties, unless they get congressional approval.

“I think the Supreme Court will be interested,” Muller said.
