HARRISBURG — Pennsylvania’s major party candidates for governor met in Harrisburg for the first and only debate of the 2026 election on Wednesday, staking out familiar positions on affordability issues, looming healthcare cuts, and education funding.

Democratic Gov. Josh Shapiro sought to tie Republican state Treasurer Stacy Garrity to Donald Trump’s administration and defended his own record, saying Pennsylvania’s economy has seen “unheard-of growth.” He repeatedly noted he can only pass what can get through the divided legislature.

Garrity cast looming Trump-backed Medicaid cuts as common sense and painted a picture of a Pennsylvania that is “falling behind.” She tried to differentiate herself from Shapiro on data centers, skill games, and education.

The race has been uneven from the start. Shapiro, a popular incumbent, <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2026/04/pennsylvania-election-governor-josh-shapiro-stacy-garrity-campaign-finance-visualized-elections/">has raised</a> about $55 million since the beginning of last year. Garrity has raised a bit over $5 million. Shapiro has dramatically outspent Garrity on advertising, and polls of the race consistently show him with a double-digit lead.

Ultimately, the one-hour debate hosted by Nexstar Media Group revealed little about the candidates that they haven&#39;t already publicly stated. The moderators did not ask any questions about energy production or the environment. They did, however, ask Shapiro about his support for Israel and how that fits into the Democratic Party and whether he&#39;d commit to serving all four years if reelected, rather than run for president.

Here are Spotlight PA’s key takeaways from the debate.

## The candidates’ clearest differences

Amid frequent critiques of each others’ records, Shapiro and Garrity staked out a few clear, diverging policy positions.

For instance, Garrity attacked Shapiro for his evolving position on school vouchers.

“The governor has held not one, not two, but three positions on school choice,” Garrity said, noting his support and then veto of a voucher program.

Shapiro replied that he is still open to vouchers, but said the voucher program he vetoed didn’t have the agreement of the Democratic-controlled House and GOP-controlled Senate.

The governor also has a final say on if Pennsylvania will enter a federal program created by Trump’s omnibus 2025 tax bill which will give taxpayers a break if they donate to private school scholarship funds and other similar programs.

“I think prudence here makes the most sense,” Shapiro said. “We&#39;re looking at it carefully, and in the next few weeks, we&#39;ll make our announcements.” Garrity, meanwhile, said he’d join the program on “day one.”

On education spending writ large, the two diverged. Garrity argued that “we can&#39;t just keep throwing more money at an issue and think you&#39;re going to get a different result” and suggested the state hold more kids back if they don’t read at grade level.

Shapiro defended his administration&#39;s education spending, noting its provided free breakfast, more vo-tech opportunities, and mental health counseling in schools.

“Graduation rates are up and truancy is down and it is working,” Shapiro said.

Garrity also reiterated her support for a flat fee on skill games, or the gambling devices that have popped up in Pennsylvania bars, restaurants, clubs and convenience stores.

Shapiro said he’d support taxing the devices at the same rate as slot machines in casinos, approximately 50%, while putting the resulting revenue into the state&#39;s coffers and “then use a core chunk of that to fund mass transit across Pennsylvania.”

### Affordability

Affordability was top of mind during the debate. The two candidates were asked about their approaches to two issues: eliminating the gas tax and increasing state funding for healthcare coverage.

On gas prices, Garrity said that she would give a rebate to Pennsylvanians during the summer months, totaling $750. She noted that Shapiro had campaigned in his first term on sending Pennsylvanians a $250 rebate for gas, but has yet to do so.

Garrity didn’t comment on whether she would support suspending the gas tax— of which Pennsylvania has one of the highest in the country.

She also said she would “unleash the energy we have under our feet,” referring to the state’s abundant natural gas.

“We are the Saudi Arabia of natural gas, so we should be producing affordable energy, and that would create 200,000 good-paying jobs; generate $5 to $6 billion in revenue,” Garrity said.

Shapiro, meanwhile, said he was open to suspending the gas tax but said “it’s up to the legislature” to bring up the measure. He blamed Trump for the high costs of gas, pointing to its tariffs and the war in Iran.

“We feel that when we go to the grocery store and see higher prices because of the tariffs, we feel the pain at the pump because of the war in Iran,” Shapiro said.

On affordability, Shapiro said he passed multiple tax cuts throughout his first term, pointing to the increase in the property tax rent rebate program for seniors and the Working Pennsylvania tax credits looped into past budget deals.

The candidates were also asked if they would direct state dollars to make up for the cuts to Medicaid enacted by the federal government.

Shapiro said that “every governor of every state is dealing” with the cuts, but that no state has the ability to make up the loss of federal funds.

Garrity, meanwhile, did not address whether she would use state funds to ensure more Pennsylvanians have coverage. But she expressed support for the federal legislation which cut the coverage, noting that the same legislation also established no tax on tips or overtime.

“I think it&#39;s common sense to require healthy adults that don&#39;t have small kids to either work, train, or volunteer 20 hours a week to make sure that Medicaid is there for the people that really need it,” Garrity said.

### Trump, Israel, Iran, and presidential aspirations

Throughout the gubernatorial race, Shapiro has argued Garrity is deferential to Trump and has sought to link her with his sweeping tariffs and upcoming Medicaid cuts. He did so again in the debate.

“When the Commonwealth of Pennsylvania put $1 in your pocket, the policies of the Trump administration — tariffs, and this war in Iran, two things that my opponents support — well, that makes it feel like they&#39;re yanking two bucks out,” Shapiro said.

### Any other new policy revelations that are less interesting

The candidates essentially agreed about whether Pennsylvania should require voters to present ID at the polls — but the issue still led to one of their testiest exchanges of the debate.

Garrity supports mandatory voter ID. And Shapiro told the moderators, “I’m open to putting a voter ID bill on my desk for my signature.” But, the governor added, that bill can&#39;t be based on “conspiracy theories” and he wants to ensure voters aren’t disenfranchised. He then claimed that Garrity couldn’t be trusted to certify the results of an election if her preferred candidate didn’t win.

“Yeah, that&#39;s the most ridiculous thing I&#39;ve ever heard,” Garrity said. “I don&#39;t even know why you&#39;d say that, and how you can look yourself in the mirror at night.”

Both candidates were asked if they’d “commit to spending public tax dollars on new stadiums to ensure” that the commonwealth’s NFL franchises stay put. Both said they were open to a deal but struck different tones.

“I don’t think we need to be giving taxpayer dollars to billionaire owners,” Garrity opened her response, but added that if owners “came and they had a proposal and it was something that we could work out and it was a win-win, certainly, I would be open to it.”

Shapiro added that he also needed to see the plans before committing taxpayer dollars, but revealed he’d already had conversations with the owners of both the Pittsburgh Steelers and Philadelphia Eagles about new stadiums.

“The bottom line here is this: I&#39;m going to keep these teams in Pennsylvania. I&#39;m going to protect taxpayer dollars, and I&#39;m going to make sure that fans have a great and affordable experience when they go to the arenas,” he said.

Garrity also repeatedly attacked Shapiro for a sexual harassment case involving a former top administration staffer, Mike Vereb.

Pressed on the incident by a moderator, Shapiro said that the investigation of the incident led to a recommendation that, should an accusation involve a high-ranking official, “the governor would be brought into that process earlier.”
