There are six old oil wells within 150 feet of Brittany and Ryan Mahoney's first house in the South Fayette neighborhood known as The Berkshires. They didn't know about any of them.

Why would they?

The thousands of oil and gas wells that still linger underground in this region are often long forgotten up on the surface. Some wells were drilled more than a century ago, and many haven't produced oil or gas in decades. Some have been plugged with tree trunks. Some are still cavities.

Many don't pose any immediate health or safety risks, but there's no easy way for residents to know if a nearby well is leaking gas, fouling groundwater, or otherwise reacting to all the other extraction activities going on underground. It's especially hard to monitor any of those hazards if you don't know where the wells are.

That's why Inside Climate News and the Pittsburgh Post-Gazette built an interactive map to bring these old ghosts to light. The tool allows you to search any location in the state and look up information about nearby wells, many of which were long since abandoned.

More than a hundred years ago, The Berkshires neighborhood was part of the most prolific oil play in the world, the McDonald Oil Field---so productive that its output was reported in gallons per minute.

Over time, farms have yielded to branching, tree-lined streets that spoke into cul-de-sacs, with names like Windsor Court and Walnut Ridge.

When the Mahoneys started to look for a new, bigger house, they wanted to stay in the neighborhood. In 2012, they learned that a piece of undeveloped land just up the street from where they lived would be going up for sale. It was their chance to build their own home---one that would eventually house their four young children and a restless chocolate lab.

### Is There an Oil or Gas Well Near Your Home?

Use the map below to search for the oil or gas well nearest to addresses in Pennsylvania. The map will display information about the well, including inspection photos where available. You can click on other wells in the map view to see information for those sites.

Exercise caution if you think a well is nearby; some may be hazardous, and many are on private property.

The well information is maintained by the Pennsylvania Department of Environmental Protection (DEP), which is responsible for data accuracy. If you have concerns about a well, contact the agency directly [through this form](https://forms.cloud.microsoft/pages/responsepage.aspx?id=QSiOQSgB1U2bbEf8Wpob3k2IRU0miSNNr_Ok_FMEdrJUMVVXMFkzSjk0RkVIWTM5NzREREQzTkZGTC4u&route=shorturl) or by calling the agency's [oil and gas office in your area](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/dep/programs-and-services/oil-and-gas/contacts-and-directions).

We simplified the DEP's descriptions of well status into four categories:

**Active/Other.** These are mostly wells that are still producing oil or gas. However, the DEP's active category includes wells with no other status description, which means some disused wells may be mistakenly included. We have also included a small number of wells with "[regulatory inactive status](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/pennsylvania/25-Pa-Code-SS-78-104)," which allows an operator to shut down production for up to five years without having to seal a well.

**Plugged** wells are recorded as sealed. Most were plugged by contractors hired by their former operators, but some were plugged by the state. Plugging may fail, so some of these wells may still leak gases.

**Abandoned/Orphaned** includes abandoned wells, which have been out of production for more than 12 months, and [orphaned](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/dep/programs-and-services/oil-and-gas/legacy-wells) wells, for which no responsible operator or owner exists.

**Historical.** In addition to its oil and gas wells [inventory](https://greenport.pa.gov/ReportExtracts/OG/OilGasWellInventoryReport), the DEP is in the process of digitizing [historical](https://www.minemaps.psu.edu/aboutnew.htm) [maps](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/dep/programs-and-services/oil-and-gas/legacy-wells/farmline-maps) showing older wells. These historical maps are not comprehensive and have largely not been verified by modern inspections. Treat this information with extra caution.

They were told that before they could start building there, someone would come and "cap" an abandoned well just behind the backyard. Brittany Mahoney's recollection is that someone did. She remembers a truck pulling up and workers clearing a path. That's all she knows.

The Pennsylvania Department of Environmental Protection, which oversees oil and gas wells and is supposed to be called to inspect well plugging activities to ensure they are done right, was never notified of any work being done behind the Mahoneys' house. Historic maps show two oil wells were drilled less than 50 feet from where a trampoline now stands in the backyard. During a recent visit, a wade through the bushes and trees just past the property line showed no evidence of a well.

"Should I be worried?" Mahoney asked when she saw the dots on a map.

In some ways, she's ahead of the pack.

"We don't have any wells here," a neighbor who answered the door two houses down said, although the interactive map that pulls well locations from DEP data and historical documents shows otherwise.

When Agnes Warner moved to The Berkshires 19 years ago, she could still see horses milling about on farmland across the street.

Abandoned wells? "That was one of the problems that never came up," she said.

Dots indicating old wells mark six homes on her street, including her own.

If you're in Pennsylvania, you too might have a dot near your home. In southwestern Pennsylvania, you could have dozens of them. So, what should you do?

A dot means the DEP, the state agency in charge of oil and gas well oversight, knows a well was drilled there at some point. Some documentation exists, even if it's just a mark on an old farmline map or coal mine blueprint. It doesn't tell you much about the health of that well.s

Many old, abandoned or orphaned wells pose no obvious danger to public health and safety. Here are some things to look for if you're concerned:

Looking for Trouble
-------------------

"Leaks is the big one," said Dan Brockett, who leads the energy education team at Penn State Extension.

"If that well is leaking anything---if it's leaking methane, if it's leaking brine water, petroleum products"---that's a red flag and should be reported to the DEP.

"In some cases, we would see some pretty severe erosion," he said, which might create a sinkhole problem.

Some old wells have been cut off at the surface; others might have rusty pipes sticking out. This could be a tripping hazard for hikers and hunters in more rural areas, or an obstacle for farmers and landscapers weaving their equipment around these relics of the past.

"The questions I would get a lot is, 'Can I just cut that? Can I just pull that?'" Brockett said. Do not attempt it, he said. You don't know if the old pipe is trapping methane.

Brockett spent the better part of last year hunting old wells with his wife in an effort funded by the Environmental Defense Fund. His mission was to get rural landowners to help document wells that the DEP may not be aware of.

The couple found about 250 wells that the DEP didn't know about---a drop in the bucket compared to the hundreds of thousands suspected wells in Pennsylvania that have yet to be discovered.

Only a few that they found had serious visible problems, Brockett said.

"We had a lot where we could see erosion. A lot of places where you could hear water running through the well," likely an indication of corroded well casing.

If he found methane leaking, most of the time he could smell a "slight petroleum odor" and see dead vegetation around the well head. Although pure methane is odorless, the gas coming out of a well can carry other substances with it.

Don't Try This at Home
----------------------

It may be tempting to try to diagnose whether a suspected abandoned well is leaking by taking the Hollywood approach---holding a lit match to the source to see if it balloons into a fireball.

Resist.

You don't know what's coming out of the well or how much pressure might be behind a leak.

Brockett said he's aware of some people trying to capture the emissions from a well in a makeshift canister. This is also dangerous.

Methane venting into the air is not necessarily a safety risk, but if it's captured and finds a spark, it could explode.

Smelling It Out
---------------

If you smell gas in or near your home, it may have nothing to do with an old well. It could be the odorant that utilities are required to inject into their gas supply for exactly this reason: to make leaks obvious.

Call your natural gas utility right away and leave the house if possible while you wait. Using a sensor, a utility worker can detect if gas is present in the air---typically in the basement---and at what level.

Gas is explosive when it comprises between 5 percent and 15 percent of the volume of air in a room. Below or above that, it will not ignite. If a gas utility detects gas at even half a percent level, it will likely shut off the supply of gas to the house---even before determining what is causing the leak.

How Well Leaks Are Found
------------------------

Fred Baldassare has been sniffing out leaking methane for more than three decades, first as a stray gas investigator with the DEP and, for the past 16 years at his firm, Echelon Applied Geochemistry Consulting. He has thousands of photos of leaking wells and what a leak might do to a home, from bubbling water wells to piles of rubble after a house explosion.

The most common way that methane issues are linked to suspected abandoned or orphaned wells is through gas utility surveys, he said.

Gas companies, like Columbia Gas or Peoples in southwestern Pennsylvania, are required to survey their pipelines for leaks. If they detect gas, they work to determine where it's coming from. If the source isn't obvious and doesn't match the chemical signature of their pipeline gas, the utility will notify the DEP, which will launch an investigation.

Mitigation
----------

The state may not always find the source, Baldassare said. Or the DEP may find a leaking well but determine it would be too hard to plug without tearing up someone's home. In those cases, the agency may install a mitigation system to drive the leaking gas away from the home.

Baldassare has put in dozens of these systems, which typically involve digging trenches around the house or, if needed, inside the ground floor, and directing the stray gas away from the residence.

When a leaking well was discovered in the basement of the first Carnegie Library ever built---the 1889 building in Braddock---plugging it wasn't an option. It was in the floor of the historic building. Now, a mitigation system vents that leak outside.

In other cases, the DEP may install vents directly on abandoned wells, some of which it will later plug, in order to route the leaking gas to the atmosphere and away from an enclosed space where it could collect and pose a safety risk. The agency has done that on two dozen occasions, mostly in southwestern Pennsylvania.

Missing Well Records
--------------------

If you know there's an old well somewhere on your property but it's not on the map, report it to the DEP.

The [form to report a suspected abandoned well](https://forms.office.com/pages/responsepage.aspx?id=QSiOQSgB1U2bbEf8Wpob3k2IRU0miSNNr_Ok_FMEdrJUMVVXMFkzSjk0RkVIWTM5NzREREQzTkZGTC4u&route=shorturl) asks for contact information, well location, whether it is visibly leaking, photos and GPS coordinates.

In Brockett's experience, a smartphone does a good job of supplying most of that data. His tip is to stand at the site for a few minutes, letting the GPS calibrate, before pulling the coordinates.

Ultimately, it will be the DEP's responsibility to determine who owns the well and to designate it as either abandoned (by an operator that may still have the responsibility to plug it) or [orphaned](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/dep/programs-and-services/oil-and-gas/legacy-wells), with no current owner and therefore a ward of the state.

In his work, Brockett often reassured farmers and other landowners who worried that reporting a well to the DEP would make them liable for plugging it.

"Finding a well on your property or uncovering a buried well does not make you responsible for the well," the DEP states.

"However," the agency adds, "attempts to produce the well, receive economic benefit, improperly ... get rid of any evidence of the well, or other careless activities can make you financially responsible for the well."

In other words, tell the DEP what you know and let the agency figure out what happens next.

The DEP prioritizes which wells are plugged based on their risk to public safety, health and the environment. It plugs a handful of old wells a year on an emergency basis. But absent an immediate hazard, it could take a while.

Get Ready for a Wait
--------------------

In the spring of 2024, Pamela Schrank's house in Murrysville briefly became a poster child of Pennsylvania's oil and gas legacy coming home to roost. She grew dizzy from the odor coming from an abandoned well in her yard, where she was gardening, and reported the well to the DEP.

Rep. Summer Lee, who had co-sponsored a bill to allocate federal funds to identify and remediate such wells, visited the Westmoreland County home and used it in her testimony in Washington. The leaking well was placed on a priority list for the DEP to plug.

Two and a half years later, Schrank is still waiting.

The well, in the corner of her backyard, is still leaking.

The money slated for plugging the well had been held up by the Trump administration---it was part of President Joe Biden's Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act---and was only recently released to the state.

The well on Schrank's property was likely drilled at the turn of the last century, she said. The DEP suspects it was plugged in the 1950s. The gas smell that becomes evident within 10-15 feet of the well is ephemeral.

"It comes and goes in waves," Schrank said.

"I'm not worried that something is going to explode," she said. But she's eager to have it dealt with.

"I would like it to be in my lifetime," she quipped.

Abandoned vs. Orphaned
----------------------

If the well you encounter is categorized by the DEP as orphaned, it will become the agency's responsibility to plug. That means there is no known owner.

But if the DEP finds an owner it believes is responsible for well, it will be classified as abandoned. The DEP will try to force that owner to plug the well in order to avoid shifting the burden to taxpayers. That could take years, if it happens at all.

The largest operating owner of abandoned wells in Pennsylvania is Alabama-based Diversified Energy Co., with thousands of wells required to be plugged. Diversified has signed consent agreements with the DEP that spread out that responsibility over 15 years.

Look for Changes
--------------------

An old well could be totally fine for decades and suddenly become a problem. Baldassare recommends looking for changes like dead vegetation or effervescing or sputtering well water.

He recalled one case where a long dormant well just outside a Fox Chapel home came alive and dumped mud and soil on the front window of the house one morning.

It could be that the casing holding pressure at bay had corroded and failed, or that something else shifted underground, pushing gas and liquids to the surface. Mining activity can also shift pressure underground.

Bad Interactions
--------------------

Given the density of old oil and gas infrastructure in southwestern Pennsylvania and the links between many of the wells and geological layers underground, sometimes plugging one well could cause others to develop problems.

The same is true for other subsurface disturbances, like drilling or fracking new wells. That's why shale gas developers are required to notify the DEP of any "communication" incidents, where their activity is suspected to impact another well.

Of the 60 reported incidents to date, the vast majority come to light because other operators detect a change in the pressure of their wells and notify the company doing an active frack. But the same thing could be happening at abandoned or orphaned wells, which aren't being actively monitored.

One such well, in New Freeport, Greene County, activated with a gush of gas and liquid in 2022, as EQT Corp. was fracking a shale gas well about a mile away. EQT reported the communication but has since come to believe it was coincident timing and denies a link.

Nevertheless, New Freeport residents with well water believe whatever happened to that abandoned well in 2022 may have impacted the aquifer from which they draw their water. They have filed a lawsuit against the company and the township supervisors declared a state of emergency last year in an effort to get a municipal water line built.

Hidden History
------------------

What lurks underground isn't just forgotten columns of steel---it's the history of the region.

"It is believed that at one time as many as 200 wells were located within the actual boundaries of McDonald Borough," reads a sign posted inside the McDonald Trail Station and History Center, where some of the walls are covered with black and white photos of derricks during the brief and spectacular 19th Century oil boom.

Many of the photos come from the personal collection of Tim Thomassy, whose family has lived in McDonald for generations. He keeps them in a box in his basement, which sits along a neat street of single family homes, about 500 feet from the nearest old well.
