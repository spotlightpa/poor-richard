HARRISBURG — Democrats’ first priority as they prepare to take full control of Pennsylvania’s government is raising the minimum wage for the first time in nearly two decades.

Such a change would likely raise pay for hundreds of thousands of workers, and Democrats say they&#39;ll move fast. “You will see the minimum wage bill reach my desk before I am sworn in to a second term,” Gov. Josh Shapiro said at an <a href="https://x.com/PAGovOffice/status/2123145461045289463?a=GpGD6n_oWtDHYYt1HmY&amp;s=20">unrelated news conference</a> last week. (His inauguration is slated for Jan. 19, two weeks after legislators are sworn in for new terms.)

But while Democrats generally agree on setting the wage at $15 an hour, they’re still figuring out other key parts of their platform on the issue — something incoming state Senate President Pro Tempore Jay Costa (D., Allegheny) acknowledged in a statement to Spotlight PA.

“While there are many details to work out about the best path forward, raising the minimum wage to $15/hour is a top priority for the 2027-28 legislative session, and we will get it done,” Costa said.

Potential disagreements could erupt over whether to raise the rate gradually or all at once, adopt an automatic annual cost of living adjustment, or allow local governments to set their own higher rates — like some hope to do in Philadelphia. Members will also need to figure out where to set tipped workers’ wages.

Plus, some members would prefer a <a href="https://www.wfmz.com/news/area/pennsylvania/pa-s-minimum-wage-debate-begins-sen--elect-pinsley-wants-25-per-hour-but/article_f70d88d2-8720-4f6f-893d-541dc308dbd0.html?utm_source=SND&amp;utm_medium=social&amp;utm_campaign=69News-X">higher rate than $15</a>.

Pennsylvania’s minimum wage is set at the federal floor of $7.25 an hour, and has been there since Congress adopted that minimum in 2009, despite all its neighboring states choosing to raise their own.

State lawmakers have tried repeatedly to raise Pennsylvania’s wage in recent years — including through <a href="https://www.pennlive.com/news/2019/11/minimum-wage-increases-passes-in-state-senate-bigger-test-will-come-in-state-house.html">a bipartisan effort</a> in 2019 — but measures always sank in the face of GOP opposition.

The state Department of Labor and Industry told Spotlight PA in a statement that it estimates a $15 minimum would directly raise wages for more than 400,000 Pennsylvanians who currently earn less.

The Keystone Research Center, a progressive think tank that has spent years calling for a minimum wage increase, estimated more than <a href="https://keystoneresearch.org/research_publication/a-not-so-sweet-16-pennsylvanias-minimum-wage-is-turning-16-years-old/">860,000</a> people could actually see raises under a $15 minimum. Their figure accounts for people who already earn that much seeing a boost after others’ wages are adjusted.

Those who oppose a minimum wage increase — including the influential <a href="https://www.pachamber.org/legislative_agenda/labor/employment_wage/minimum_wage/">Pennsylvania Chamber of Business and Industry</a> — often argue raising it could hurt small businesses who rely on lower-paying labor, reduce the number of <a href="https://www.documentcloud.org/documents/28926982-revenue-proposal-analysis-2025-03-1/#document/p18">working hours</a> available, and inflate the costs of goods and services.

Proponents of a higher minimum <a href="https://pennpolicy.org/research_publication/why-we-should-raise-the-minimum-wage-in-pennsylvania-to-15-per-hour/">argue</a> the federal rate is simply too low for people to be able to afford basics like rent, groceries, and raising a family. They also argue a higher wage allows workers to spend more and contribute more to the economy.

And the idea is popular. A <a href="https://poll.qu.edu/images/polling/pa/pa02252026_pdkl34.pdf">February Quinnipiac University Poll</a> found that roughly two-thirds of registered voters in Pennsylvania support a $15 minimum wage.

## What’s on the table?

While Democrats haven’t yet provided details about what their minimum wage increase will look like, leaders’ past unsuccessful proposals hint at what Pennsylvanians might be able to expect.

Members of the party hold a wide range of positions. That’s important, particularly in the closely divided state Senate, because Democrats can only afford to lose a single vote in that chamber before it deadlocks.

{{<youtube id="LsKElBhDvqs" loading="lazy">}}

<a href="https://www.pahouse.com/InTheNews/NewsRelease/?id=142879">In March</a>, state House Democrats passed a bill that would have incrementally reached $15 an hour by 2029 with a cost of living adjustment — first setting the wage floor at $11 in 2027, then $13 in 2028. For tipped workers, the legislation would have set the state minimum at 60% of the regular minimum wage.

The measure would have allowed counties to expedite their timeline to reach $15. But it explicitly prohibited them from adopting a higher wage floor than $15. The bill never passed out of committee in the GOP-led state Senate.

Without Republicans in power, however, bills that allow local governments to raise their wages may be on the table. That would require removing Pennsylvania’s minimum wage preemption law, which <a href="https://billypenn.com/2018/07/10/pennsylvanias-minimum-wage-is-7-25-an-hour-does-it-need-to-change/">since 2006</a> has required any change to the wage to be made at the state level.

Last year, key state Senate Democrats backed a <a href="https://www.palegis.us/legislation/bills/2025/sb19">measure</a> from Sen. Christine Tartaglione (D., Philadelphia), a longtime advocate for a minimum wage increase, that would have allowed local governments to set a higher wage. Those backers included Costa and incoming Senate Appropriations Chair Vincent Hughes (D., Philadelphia) — both of whom will soon be key <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2026/11/pennsylvania-house-senate-democratic-leaders-trifecta-mcclinton-bradford-costa-collett-capitol/">chamber leaders</a>.

Still, giving more power to local governments is not a certainty.

“I don’t want to be in a position where I am negotiating or writing legislation in an interview, but I think we’ll get higher wages for workers in Philadelphia,” state Rep. Malcolm Kenyatta (D., Philadelphia), told Spotlight PA. Kenyatta also serves as a Democratic National Committee vice chair.

Another <a href="https://www.palegis.us/legislation/bills/2025/hb1150">state House bill,</a> backed by some of the party’s progressive flank, would have set the rate at $15 an hour at the start of the next year, tied an annual increase to inflation, and given municipalities the opportunity to set their rates higher. It never cleared a committee vote.

An immediate raise to $15 is the approach the Keystone Research Center favors.

And automatic boosts “would ensure that, you know, the buying power is preserved for the lowest wage workers in Pennsylvania, but also that the legislature doesn&#39;t have to revisit this issue year after year, as they&#39;ve done … since 2009,” said Claire Kovach, research director at the center.

Even some Republicans had a minimum wage bill — one that would raise the rate to <a href="https://www.palegis.us/senate/co-sponsorship/memo?memoID=47169&amp;document=SB1093">$11 an hour by 2028</a> — though many in the party opposed an increase and state Senate leaders had been unwilling to bring a proposal to a vote in recent years.

State Sen. Dan Laughlin (R., Erie) introduced the $11 proposal as a “meaningful first step that avoids the sticker shock” to small businesses that he said he worries an increase to $15 would cause.

“There was also a political reality I had to navigate,” Laughlin told Spotlight PA. “I needed to build Republican support in the Senate, and $15 an hour right out of the gate was a nonstarter. My goal was to put forward a realistic proposal that could gain bipartisan support and actually get across the finish line.”

The economic concerns were on display when lawmakers debated a minimum wage hike in the state House <a href="https://www.youtube.com/live/eo0fTUqTjdw?si=yCGH9eXVwP2_yyWU&amp;t=20069">in March</a>. Minority Leader Jesse Topper (R., Bedford) said he believed it would spike prices and harm the “future workforce.”

“I do believe that the jobs that will be eliminated will not necessarily be the jobs that would flow to other states,” Topper said. “They would be the jobs of our young high school students who are learning how to work, who are learning with hands-on training how to get to work on time, how to interact with people.”

Impacts on small businesses aren&#39;t just a Republican concern. Sen. Art Haywood, a Democrat representing Montgomery County, proposed a two-year tax credit last year to temporarily offset some of the costs of a higher minimum and “ease” the transition of raising the wage. He told Spotlight PA his pitch — based on a program <a href="https://www.ilga.gov/ftp/JCAR/AdminCode/086/086001000V73900R.html">Illinois adopted in 2020</a> — “will now be seriously considered” as part of the policy debate.
