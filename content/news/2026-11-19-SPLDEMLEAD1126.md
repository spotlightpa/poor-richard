HARRISBURG — Democrats have chosen who will lead Pennsylvania&#39;s state legislature as the party navigates its first trifecta in decades.

Many of these leaders have decades of Harrisburg experience, but they&#39;ll face a new challenge next session: Lawmaking is different when you control the state House, Senate, and governor&#39;s office.

Without an automatic check on priorities from a GOP-controlled state Senate, Democrats will be able to unilaterally cut deals behind closed doors, decide which bills are brought to a vote, pick who chairs and sits on legislative committees, and work to keep their members unified on tough issues.

This means the personalities, priorities, and lawmaking styles of chamber leaders matter a lot.

{{<embed/newsletter>}}

While both chambers’ slates are dominated by tested lawmakers, even the longest-serving state senators have never led from the majority. Most of the key figures in both chambers are from the southeast, and have reputations for being pragmatists.

In the state House, Democratic leaders will go from leading a narrow majority to — barring changes from <a href="https://lancasteronline.com/news/politics/voters-petition-court-for-ballot-box-checks-in-pennsylvania-s-41st-house-district-race/article_66c95ae6-cd7c-44db-814f-9fa625d981a0.html">a recount in one race</a> — a 13-seat one. The current legislative session has seen the chamber pass bills on the <a href="https://www.palegis.us/legislation/bills/2025/hb2189">minimum wage</a>, <a href="https://www.palegis.us/legislation/bills/2025/hb1788">public transit funding</a>, and <a href="https://www.palegis.us/legislation/bills/2025/hb1593">firearm ownership</a>, only to see them die in the Republican-led Senate.

The majority will be much narrower in the state Senate, where the party will be able to lose just a single Democratic vote before the chamber deadlocks. That caucus also has wide ideological divides.

Legislators will be working with a <a href="https://poll.qu.edu/poll-release?releaseid=3933">popular</a> Democratic governor, Josh Shapiro, who is also considered a likely <a href="https://www.inquirer.com/politics/pennsylvania/josh-shapiro-democrats-conference-presidential-candidates-20261113.html">2028 presidential candidate.</a>

A party trifecta “shines more of a spotlight on the potential gatekeepers” within state government, such as the top legislative leaders and the governor, because they must decide “how far they want to go and how fast,” said Alex Garlick, a University of Vermont political science professor who studies state legislatures.

That means debates will no longer be partisan affairs in which opponents point fingers at the other for blocking priorities; discussions will more likely be about policy, on the merits.

“Liberals want to pick their spots, and moderates want to pick their spots,” Garlick added. “I don&#39;t think it always goes one way or the other, but it changes the tension from between parties to often within the party.”

Democratic leaders have projected unity, announcing that in the first 100 days of the new legislative session they’ll work with Shapiro to raise the minimum wage, codify abortion access, increase funding for mass transit, and more. But they also face pressure to address a host of other issues, such as <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2026/11/pennsylvania-data-centers-democrats-senate-environment/">data center regulation</a>, <a href="https://www.abc27.com/pennsylvania-politics/legalizing-marijuana-not-a-top-priority-incoming-pennsylvania-senate-leader-says/">legalizing recreational cannabis</a>, and high-profile state Supreme Court rulings <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2026/06/skill-games-illegal-pennsylvania-supreme-court-ruling-capitol/">outlawing skill games</a> and deeming the commonwealth’s <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2026/03/pennsylvania-felony-second-degree-murder-supreme-court-ruling-justice-system/">sentencing scheme for second-degree murder</a> unconstitutional.

Of the elected caucus positions, a handful carry the most weight: majority leader, appropriations chair, and whip, as well as House speaker and Senate president pro tempore.

{{<bio-nav thumb-size="84">}}

Their exact roles differ depending on each chamber and the internal dynamics and personalities of each leadership team. But broadly, the majority leader — or floor leader — leads most negotiations, sets the voting calendar, and controls caucus budgets. The appropriations chair oversees legislation on state spending and helps cut budget deals. The whip is tasked with maintaining party discipline on votes. The presiding officers for the state House and Senate, the speaker and president pro tempore, respectively, decide when to call up individual bills for votes, control the daily debates, select committee chairs, and serve as institutional figureheads. (All are also <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/series/access-harrisburg/">key campaign fundraisers</a>.)

Most of these caucus leaders are elected in internal, closed-door elections by members of their chamber and party. The House speaker and Senate president pro tempore are each picked in a public floor vote of the entire chamber after nominees are chosen in internal caucus votes.

Here’s what we know about their records ahead of the new session’s official start in January.

## Familiar leaders, bigger majority in state House

{{<picture caption="The state House&#39;s top officer, Joanna McClinton, speaks at a post-2026 midterm news conference." credit="Commonwealth Media Services" description="The state House&#39;s top officer, Joanna McClinton, speaks at a post-2026 midterm news conference." src="2026/11/01nf-s6zx-f3xk-t4cb.jpeg">}}

House Democrats’ leadership team didn’t change much this year.

Its most prominent players are all from eastern Pennsylvania, and are the same people who kept the caucus’s razor-thin majority unified on key policy issues over the past four years.

With more members, the caucus now has some breathing room. But the combination of a big, diverse majority and a dozen-plus new faces can present its own challenges.

The state House Republican leaders who will serve as a minority counterpoint are also mostly unchanged, including Minority Leader Jesse Topper (Bedford). However, state Rep. Jamie Barton (Schuylkill) will replace Rep. Jim Struzzi (Indiana) as Appropriations Committee chair.

{{<bio group="House" src="2026/11/01nf-y6hy-g8nn-4xr6.jpeg" name="Joanna McClinton" role="Speaker of the House" party="Democrat, Philadelphia" credit="Commonwealth Media Services" focus="fp:0.5:0.3">}}

State House Speaker Joanna McClinton (D., Philadelphia) is entering her third legislative session in the chamber’s top position.

McClinton, 44, is an attorney who served as a public defender and state Senate counsel before winning a 2015 special election to her West Philadelphia-centered district.

She rose quickly through the state House ranks. McClinton’s peers elected her caucus chair in 2018 as part of a <a href="https://www.pennlive.com/news/2018/11/pa-house-democrats-revamp-leadership-team-with-philly-tilt.html">regional shakeup</a>, then minority leader in 2020 after the <a href="https://penncapital-star.com/government-politics/after-year-of-tough-votes-dermody-loses-reelection-clearing-way-for-democratic-free-for-all/">previous leader lost reelection</a>.

As floor leader, McClinton played a key role in the 2021-2022 round of redistricting <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2022/02/pennsylvania-redistricting-legislative-maps-final-vote/">that gave state House Democrats a more winnable map</a> —&nbsp;pushing, for instance, to <a href="https://penncapital-star.com/government-politics/mcclinton-calls-for-safe-guards-against-prison-gerrymandering-in-pa-legislative-maps/">count people in prison at their former home addresses</a> rather than in their cells. This slightly shrank the constituencies of the rural districts where most prisons are located.

She first took the speaker’s rostrum in 2023 — becoming Pennsylvania’s first woman and second Black person to do so — after a protracted battle for control of the chamber. When Democrats won a bare majority in the 2022 election, vacancies left the party without its full complement for months, and they temporarily elected a different speaker to appease Republicans. McClinton, the caucus’s top choice, <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2023/02/pa-house-speaker-mark-rozzi-resigns/">took over in February 2023</a>.

The next session, Democrats adopted new compromise rules, giving the minority party more sway over legislation by shrinking committee rosters. This <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2025/01/legislature-pennsylvania-2025-rules-harrisburg-house-senate-session/">avoided a messy floor fight</a> on the first day of session.

Before and during her time as speaker, McClinton <a href="https://www.inquirer.com/politics/pennsylvania-crown-act-20251120.html">championed</a> the CROWN Act, a measure outlawing discrimination based on an individual’s natural hair, which was signed into law last year. She has led election policy, sponsoring a sweeping <a href="https://www.palegis.us/legislation/bills/2025/hb1396">bill</a> that would have allowed in-person early voting and same-day registration. The measure passed the state House in 2025 but died in the Senate, and Democrats <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2026/11/election-law-legislature-priorities-democrats-trifecta-pennsylvania-elections/">have said they intend to revive</a> the issue.

McClinton often cites her faith when guiding the chamber’s votes and procedures.

“For me, it’s a little bit more of a way to unify, and to learn and to most importantly be able to understand,” she <a href="https://penncapital-star.com/government-politics/capstar-qa-pa-house-democratic-leader-joanna-mcclinton-on-bipartisanship-redistricting/">said</a> in 2020 of her relationship with religion.

{{<bio group="House" src="2026/11/01nf-y7ab-fmh2-0ycj.jpeg" name="Matt Bradford" role="House Majority Leader" party="Democrat, Montgomery" credit="Commonwealth Media Services" focus="fp:0.6:0.3">}}

First elected to the state House in 2008, Rep. Matt Bradford (D., Montgomery), 51, rose to prominence in the caucus as chair of the House State Government Committee, dealing with issues like election and redistricting policy. (He once made <a href="https://www.nbcnews.com/politics/politics-news/pennsylvania-lawmaker-scolds-male-colleague-during-meeting-i-m-heterosexual-n826981">national headlines</a> when his Republican counterpart on that committee <a href="https://whyy.org/articles/tirade-touch-arm-pa-capitol-continues-second-day/">accused him of being gay</a> for touching his arm.)

In 2018, Bradford participated in a successful bipartisan effort to <a href="https://billypenn.com/2018/06/26/whats-behind-the-fight-over-pennsylvania-redistricting-reform/">block a state Senate GOP redistricting bill, which Democratic critics said would attack the Pennsylvania judiciary.</a>

And after the 2018 midterms saw Democrats gain a dozen seats in Philadelphia and its suburbs, Bradford helped organize his fellow southeastern legislators to win a majority of posts in caucus leadership, shifting political power within the House Democratic caucus away from the western half of the state.

He has been majority leader since 2023. In that time, Bradford has been charged with keeping Democrats’ slim majority together in tough spots.

During bargaining over the 2023 budget, for instance, he was the public face <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2023/06/pa-private-school-choice-vouchers-josh-shapiro-legislature-budget-talks/">opposing an alleged deal</a> between Shapiro’s administration and state Senate Republicans to pass a voucher program. Shapiro <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2023/07/pennsylvania-budget-pass-house-senate-shapiro-education/">ultimately vetoed</a> the voucher provision from that year’s spending package after Senate Republicans had already passed the legislation from their chamber. Shapiro, who supported <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2024/07/josh-shapiro-vice-president-school-choice-voucher/">vouchers during his 2022 campaign</a>, denied there was a deal to begin with.

In the years since, Bradford, who is known for <a href="https://x.com/StephenJ_Caruso/status/1694767569142436009?a=lHD9I12IOLYLmLDt4w4&amp;s=20">cracking jokes</a> and arriving at the Capitol in a Phillies cap and gym shorts, has championed a pragmatic approach to managing the state House’s narrow majority.

He noted in 2023, after a failed vote on a Democratic bill to require reporting of lost firearms, “At 102, we’re going to have to be bipartisan and thoughtful and strategic.” He has, for his part, offered to engineer deals with state Senate Republicans, such as <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2023/10/pennsylvania-2024-presidential-primary-date-legislation-change-house/">tightening voter ID requirements</a> (that effort failed).

In recent public remarks, Bradford has indicated his approach won’t change even with 114 votes. “We have a mandate, yes. But it&#39;s a mandate for what we talked about when we were at 102 — humility,” <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2026/11/pennsylvania-democrats-trifecta-agenda-minimum-wage-capitol/">he said</a>.

Back in Montgomery County, Bradford has played a central role in <a href="https://www.inquirer.com/politics/election/montgomery-county-pa-democratic-commissioner-race-20230501.html">influencing which candidates run in</a> local races. An attorney, he continues to work as counsel for <a href="https://www.cgbaglaw.com/our-team">CGBAG Law</a>.

{{<bio group="House" src="2026/11/01nf-y6x5-6vdb-5sen.jpeg" name="Jordan Harris" role="House Appropriations Chair" party="Democrat, Philadelphia" credit="Commonwealth Media Services" focus="fp:0.5:0.3">}}

State Rep. <a href="https://penncapital-star.com/education/bipartisanship-is-passe-no-one-told-jordan-harris/">Jordan Harris</a> (D., Philadelphia), 42, chairs the panel responsible for introducing and approving Pennsylvania’s annual spending plans, which direct upward of $50 billion in state spending.

Harris’ frequent press gaggles in the state House hallway after Appropriations Committee meetings have made him a consistent messenger for the party, especially in June when budget talks ramp up. He habitually quotes hip-hop and R&amp;B lyrics on the House floor, particularly while selling spending deals, and is often charged with brushing off questions the caucus isn’t ready to answer.

For instance, asked in June if lawmakers had gotten any closer to a deal on potential revenue generators like legalizing recreational cannabis, Harris responded with a non sequitur: “That&#39;s a nice T-shirt. Where do you get your T-shirts from?” (He then said his caucus was still in conversations with state Senate Republicans.)

A former Philadelphia school teacher, Harris first won his state House seat in 2012, and served as caucus whip before taking over as Appropriations Committee chair in 2023.

He frequently stresses the importance of funding public education, and also helped lead efforts to adopt and expand Pennsylvania’s <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2023/12/pennsylvania-criminal-justice-clean-slate-probation-legislature-crime-septa/">Clean Slate Law</a>, which automatically seals certain criminal records so they can’t limit people’s opportunities for employment, housing, and education.

Harris has also defended Pennsylvania’s current ethics law, which allows members to accept unlimited gifts and hospitality so long as they report ones over a certain threshold. In 2019 he <a href="https://www.nbcphiladelphia.com/news/local/pennsylvania-lawmaker-gifts-ethics-disclosures/172613/">said of lobbyist-funded meals</a>, “There&#39;s a tension at the meeting table that doesn&#39;t happen at the dinner table. And it&#39;s because of that that people are actually able to get things done.&#34;

{{<bio group="House" src="2026/11/01nf-y61z-j475-4v2c.jpeg" name="Mike Schlossberg" role="House Majority Whip" party="Democrat, Lehigh" credit="Commonwealth Media Services">}}

Since 2025, state Rep. Mike Schlossberg (D., Lehigh) has served as majority whip, tasked with hearing House Democrats’ concerns over legislation and ensuring they vote the party line.

That role could become more difficult as caucus membership grows on swearing-in day, and more rank-and-file members may feel emboldened to break from the party.

Schlossberg, 43, was an Allentown City Council member and worked at the Greater Lehigh Valley Chamber of Commerce before winning his seat in 2012.

He is among the legislature’s most vocal advocates for supporting mental health, and has said he has lived with <a href="https://mikeschlossbergauthor.com/about/">depression and anxiety-related disorders</a> since he was a teenager.

Schlossberg has authored two novels and a book for politicians about avoiding a “career-ending mistake” on social media.

## A new era for longtime state senators

{{<picture caption="The state Senate&#39;s incoming top officer, Jay Costa, speaks at a post-2026 midterms news conference." credit="Commonwealth Media Services" description="The state Senate&#39;s incoming top officer, Jay Costa, speaks at a post-2026 midterms news conference." src="2026/11/01nf-s706-thy1-pv5j.jpeg">}}

While Senate Democrats’ leadership team has seen some reshuffling, most of the faces are Harrisburg veterans. Two of the top four officers have a combined 70 years of experience in the Capitol’s halls.

The team will need to leverage that experience to manage Democrats’ first state Senate majority in more than 30 years. The caucus includes a younger progressive wing and an old guard of moderates. With a 26-24 majority, they can afford to lose only a single member before they stop being able to pass bills. (Democratic Lt. Gov. Austin Davis presides over the chamber, but can break ties only on procedural votes, not final passage.)

Senate Republicans are seeing their own changes. In caucus elections Tuesday, current President Pro Tempore Kim Ward (Westmoreland) did not seek leadership again. That left Sen. Joe Pittman (Indiana) uncontested to take over as minority leader, after serving as majority leader since 2022. Other GOP leaders will remain in place, like Sen. Wayne Langerholc (Cambria) as whip and Sen. Scott Martin (Lancaster) as appropriations chair.

{{<bio group="Senate" src="2026/11/01nf-y70t-9g1p-a58t.jpeg" name="Jay Costa" role="Senate President Pro Tempore" party="Democrat, Allegheny" credit="Commonwealth Media Services" focus="fp:0.5:0.3">}}

State Sen. Jay Costa, 69, has served three decades in the Senate and has led his caucus since 2010. But he has always done so from the minority.

He’ll take the reins of the chamber as president pro tempore, appointing committee chairs and overseeing floor proceedings when the lieutenant governor is absent.

The scion of a Pittsburgh political family, Costa worked as a janitor, bartender, deputy sheriff, lawyer, and county row officer before winning legislative office in a 1996 special election. His brother and cousin have both served alongside him in the legislature, but both lost primaries to more progressive candidates in 2018. (His brother and son are now lobbyists.)

Costa has consistently introduced bills that would limit campaign donations, expand access to healthcare, and add judicial branch employees to the list of protected staff under the Whistleblower Law. Some of his most significant efforts have been focused on his home city of Pittsburgh, including bills to consolidate local services <a href="https://www.butlereagle.com/20250428/state-senator-proposes-multicounty-transit-authority/">like transit</a>, and let the city <a href="https://senatorcosta.com/loop/">exempt longtime homeowners</a> from property taxes.

Costa’s rise into the highest rungs of the state Senate’s Democratic caucus began in 2008, after former Sen. Vince Fumo (D., Philadelphia), a powerful figure, resigned amid <a href="https://www.justice.gov/archive/usao/pae/News/2007/feb/FumoPressRelease2.6.07.pdf">a federal investigation</a>. The Philadelphia Daily News reported at the time that Costa managed to snag Fumo’s position as minority appropriations chair after the Philadelphia delegation splintered into factions.

In 2010, after Northeast Pennsylvania state Sen. Bob Mellow retired, also amid a federal investigation, Costa succeeded him as minority leader.

Costa has been a vocal critic of Republican governance, speaking out against budget cuts from 2011 to 2015, when the GOP held a trifecta, and opposing everything from GOP-initiated <a href="https://whyy.org/articles/chaos-erupts-in-pennsylvania-senate-over-welfare-bill/">benefit reductions</a> to <a href="https://penncapital-star.com/election-2020/pa-senate-democrats-file-suit-to-block-gop-subpoenas-in-2020-election-investigation/">investigations into the 2020 election</a>.

But Costa and Senate Democrats have struggled to actually retake power in the upper chamber, which has been under near-continuous GOP control since 1980.

The caucus’s hopes were dashed repeatedly. “We’re really excited,” Costa told the Pittsburgh Post-Gazette about Democrats’ electoral prospects in July 2014, when the body was 27-23 Republican. “We’re really right on the cusp.” Democrats went on to lose three seats that year, before descending to just 16 seats in 2016 — their low mark before regaining seats throughout the Trump era.

Costa still works at Dickie, McCamey &amp; Chilcote P.C., a Pittsburgh-based law firm that lists his specialties as “public finance, municipal law, government, and estate planning and administration.”

{{<bio group="Senate" src="2026/11/01nf-y72m-byjf-wse8.jpeg" name="Maria Collett" role="Senate Majority Leader" party="Democrat, Montgomery" credit="Commonwealth Media Services" focus="fp:0.5:0.3">}}

State Sen. Maria Collett (D., Montgomery) is the freshest face in chamber leadership. She first won office in 2018, flipping a historically Republican district in the Philadelphia suburbs.

Born in Athens, Greece, Collett, 52, is a former trauma nurse and deputy attorney general of Camden County, New Jersey. She has quietly worked her way into the top ranks of the caucus in recent years, and won the office of majority leader unopposed.

She sponsored dozens of bills this session, many on healthcare, privacy rights, and caregiving, including a <a href="https://www.palegis.us/legislation/bills/2025/sb906">bill</a> that aimed to create a statewide paid family and medical leave program funded by employee contributions and administered by the state Department of Labor and Industry. Despite having a Republican co-sponsor, it didn’t pass the upper chamber.

But that wasn’t the only bill on which Collett worked with Republicans. She also collaborated on a 2026 <a href="https://www.palegis.us/legislation/bills/2025/sb0045">law</a> that stiffened penalties for people who traffic and patronize sex workers, and on <a href="https://senatorward.com/2023/11/14/senate-takes-steps-to-advance-bipartisan-sexual-harassment-legislation/">the Senate GOP’s legislative response</a> to high-profile sexual harassment claims in state government — including one that led to the 2023 resignation of <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2023/09/pennsylvania-mike-vereb-sexual-harassment-allegation-josh-shapiro-administration/">a top Shapiro aide</a> and another that led to a <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2023/03/pa-mike-zabel-sexual-harassment-resigns/">House member’s resignation</a>.

She summarized her legislative approach in a <a href="https://www.senatorcollett.com/sen-collett-talks-paid-leave-sexual-harassment-policy-at-us-dept-of-labor-panel-in-washington/">2024 statement</a>.

“The barriers exist,” Collett said. “It’s my job to figure out a way to get around them, to break them down, or to go under them if I have to - whatever it takes to find allies that are able to advocate for this piece of legislation that I know can do good things for the people of Pennsylvania.”

Like Bradford, Collett is counsel at Montgomery County law firm <a href="https://www.cgbaglaw.com/our-team">CGBAG Law</a>.

{{<bio group="Senate" src="2026/11/01nf-y73y-ajcw-xn13.jpeg" name="Vincent Hughes" role="Senate Appropriations Chair" party="Democrat, Philadelphia" credit="Commonwealth Media Services">}}

State Sen. Vincent Hughes, 70, will mark his 40th anniversary in Harrisburg next year.

After being elected to the state House in 1986, Hughes captured his Senate seat in 1994, which covers parts of Montgomery County and North and West Philadelphia. (He briefly worked as a library administrator at the University of Pennsylvania before winning office.)

Hughes has been a consistent voice for Philadelphia in the Capitol over the years, calling for increased state funding for education, including teacher training and school building construction. He is a proponent of social safety net programs, and initiatives to help community groups fight violence. In 2018, he helped create <a href="https://www.palegis.us/senate/co-sponsorship/memo?memoID=25520&amp;document=SB1142">a statewide school safety hotline</a>.

Hughes is also a former chair of the Pennsylvania Legislative Black Caucus, and newspaper accounts throughout the early 1990s frequently detailed him using his bully pulpit to call out racism and discrimination. In 2021, <a href="https://www.witf.org/2020/06/29/two-for-the-price-of-one-pair-of-proposed-amendments-to-state-constitution-head-to-pa-house/">he successfully put</a> a constitutional amendment adding protections against racial discrimination on the ballot. It passed.

Known for delivering impassioned speeches on the Senate floor and at news conferences, Hughes has sponsored a long list of initiatives that haven&#39;t moved in the GOP-controlled chamber, from a plan to give students from low- and middle-income households <a href="https://www.senatorhughes.com/papromise/">free tuition to state colleges</a>, to legislation that would restrict access to firearms and require transparency in drug pricing.

Hughes has chaired the Appropriations Committee since 2010. He and his wife, <em>Abbott Elementary</em> star Sheryl Lee Ralph, often attend Democratic fundraisers and other initiatives around Philadelphia together — the two recently hosted a literacy summit in Hughes’ district.

{{<bio group="Senate" src="2026/11/01nf-y77c-w9ka-4nvw.jpeg" name="Steve Santarsiero" role="Senate Majority Whip" party="Democrat, Bucks" credit="Commonwealth Media Services" focus="fp:0.4:0.3">}}

State Sen. Steve Santarsiero, 61, is a former teacher, township supervisor, and state deputy attorney general for environmental protection. He’ll be majority whip, tasked with maintaining vote counts and rounding up members to support party priorities. Previously, he served as the caucus’ secretary.

Santarsiero first won a state House seat in 2008. He led an unsuccessful bid for Congress in 2016, before winning his Bucks County Senate seat in 2018.

This session, he helped push the state’s new statewide school cellphone ban, which was backed by Shapiro and other top Democrats. He also proposed an <a href="https://www.palegis.us/senate/co-sponsorship/memo?memoID=44672">assault weapon ban</a>, <a href="https://www.palegis.us/senate/co-sponsorship/memo?memoID=45171">stricter rules for natural gas drilling</a>, and <a href="https://www.palegis.us/senate/co-sponsorship/memo?memoID=46788">anti-discrimination rules for LGBTQ individuals</a> — measures that never advanced in the GOP-controlled Senate. In 2024, he also <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2024/06/pennsylvania-colleges-universities-israel-divestment-boycott-ban-legislature/">introduced a bipartisan bill</a> to strip state-funding from state colleges and universities that divested from Israel in response to public backlash against the civilian death toll in Israel’s war in Gaza.

Santarsiero has said he first grew interested in public service after watching the 9/11 terrorist attack on the World Trade Center from an office window in Newark, New Jersey. Subsequently, he took night classes at Holy Family University to earn his teaching certification.

Like Bradford and Collett, he is counsel at CGBAG Law. He also chairs the Bucks County Democratic Committee.<strong></strong>
