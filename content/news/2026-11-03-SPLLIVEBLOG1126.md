{{<card hed="A ‘conservative’ voter for Shapiro" timestamp="2026-11-03T09:19:00-05:00">}}
Nestled behind the Susquehanna River sits the Lock Haven Regatta Foundation building. A small “Vote Here” sign in the back alley leads voters to a basement.

Brett Everhart, a professor at Commonwealth University-Lock Haven, lives on Water Street. He made his way to the building to vote just past 7 a.m.

Everhart said he is conservative but doesn’t always vote along the party line.

“It’s \[about\] who’s the best one,” he told the News Lab at Penn State. Adding that he supports Democratic Gov. Josh Shapiro, who is seeking reelection against Republican challenger state Treasurer Stacy Garrity. “I think that’s a big deal right now,” he said.

Everhart said he is “pretty pleased” with how his family and his city are doing. “So I am not looking for a whole lot of change,” he said.

National issues are nothing that “one person can solve right now,” Everhart said. He thinks the nation has to be patient and let things “take care of themselves eventually.” <em>—⁠Avery McGurgan, The News Lab at Penn State </em>

{{<card hed="Splitting the ticket in Delaware County" timestamp="2026-11-03T09:09:00-05:00">}}
Charles Vogeley, a 75-year-old accountant in Concord Township, Delaware County, has been a registered Republican for as long as he&#39;s been voting.

Still, he split his ticket this year. He voted for Gov. Josh Shapiro, who Vogeley thinks works hard and has a &#34;good head on his shoulders.&#34; He didn&#39;t even know the name of Shapiro’s GOP opponent, Stacy Garrity, and said he can&#39;t remember the last time he voted for a Republican for governor.

Still, Vogeley voted for his local GOP state Rep. Craig Williams.

Williams is &#34;doing as good a job as he can do,&#34; Vogeley said. In particular, Vogeley appreciated Williams’ focus on utility prices, which Vogeley thinks were driven high by data centers.

Williams&#39; district, the 160th in a wealthy corner of the Philadelphia suburbs, is a top target for Democrats as they push to expand their control of the state House.

Shapiro has personally stumped for Democrat Elizabeth Moro as part of his efforts to win the vaunted trifecta — control of both legislative chambers and the executive — and hopefully pass key policy wins without having to negotiate with Republicans.

But it&#39;s that very frictionlessness that Vogeley says he fears, and which informed his ticket splitting.

&#34;The split legislature is OK because it provides checks and balances,&#34; he said. Under unified party control, &#34;it can become a steamroller.&#34; <em>—Stephen Caruso </em>

{{<card hed="A first-time voter at 63" image="cas/4d1z-91pz-zbxq-v0jj.jpeg" image-credit="Avery McGurgan / The News Lab at Penn State" image-description="Darin Bumbaco, 63, votes for the first time on Nov. 3, 2026, in Troy Township, Pa." timestamp="2026-11-03T08:55:00-05:00">}}
Darin Bumbaco, 63, a disabled retiree, cast his first-ever ballot last week in Troy Township. The Towanda native walked, cane in hand, from his home to Trinity Lutheran Church after polls opened on Tuesday morning to collect his “I Voted” sticker.

For Bumbaco, this election cycle represents a break from the norm, for himself and, hopefully, from the country’s politicians.

“That’s why I never really voted,” Bumbaco told the News Lab at Penn State. “Every election, these politicians promise and promise. Nothing ever gets done.”

Economic upheaval and growing racial divides over the last two years finally urged Bumbaco to the polls.

“The cost of living goes up 100% every year, and the salaries never go up,” he said. “I could go back to work today and make probably $3 more an hour than I would have made 20 years ago. How crazy is that?”

Standing on the pavement in his black jacket and beanie, Bumbaco stands a bit taller having contributed to the democratic process for the first time.

“I wanted to vote this year so I can at least say I wasn’t one of those guys that complained about everything and didn’t get his vote in to try and change something, even if it doesn’t get changed,” said Bumbaco. “ I did my part.” <em>—⁠Avery McGurgan, The News Lab at Penn State </em>

{{<card hed="A Democratic lawmaker’s cautious optimism" timestamp="2026-11-03T08:46:00-05:00">}}
Democratic state Rep. Nate Davidson, running for a second term this year, came to West Creek Hills Elementary School in Cumberland County’s East Pennsboro Township to greet voters.

The polling place, where voters from two precincts cast ballots, is one of the largest in his district.

He said he felt optimistic about his race, as well as that of Gov. Josh Shapiro, who is also up for reelection.

“It’s the other races,” he said, his voice trailing off for a minute. He was referring to the national picture, where control of Congress is front and center with multiple hugely competitive races for U.S. Senate seats, from Texas to Alaska to North Carolina.

“Am I supposed to believe polls? The last one I saw, the national generic is D-plus-10,” he said, adding that those numbers are reminiscent of years Democrats swept in midterm races. “I am anxiously optimistic.”

When he was campaigning, he said affordability issues were on voters’ minds, as was “corruption” in Washington, D.C.

“It just all kind of ties together with data centers, and corruption, and oligarchs, and ‘costs are going up for me but not for others,’” he said. “And I think a lot of voters are connecting the dots.”

Another big issue he’s heard from voters has not been in the national conversation as of late, but still apparently looms large: former financier and convicted sex offender Jeffrey Epstein.

When President Donald Trump was running for president in 2024, he promised to release a trove of law enforcement files on Epstein. After he was elected, however, his administration came under scrutiny for missing key deadlines for releasing the case files; and as some of those files were released by the Justice Department in the months after, they <a href="https://www.nytimes.com/2026/02/01/us/trump-epstein-files.html">contained multiple references to the president</a>, who had been a close friend of Epstein’s.

“You can’t run for president saying you are going to release the Epstein files, learn that you are in them a lot, and then cover them up,” he said. “Oligarchs are in them too. And I think voters are connecting those dots.” <em>—Angela Couloumbis </em>

{{<card hed="What’s motivating a Bradford County voter" timestamp="2026-11-03T08:18:00-05:00">}}
Afton Camp, 40, worked the night shift at the Qnity manufacturing plant in Towanda, Bradford County, before stopping to vote this morning. She cast her ballot before 7:30 a.m., as she does in every election, at Trinity Lutheran Church before heading home to sleep.

She hopes her neighbors will get out and vote today, too.

“It can’t just rely on one or two people,” Camp told the News Lab at Penn State. “If anybody wants change, they have to help make the change.”

Camp said she wants to elect the candidates that best represent the people because she has seen politicians becoming distanced from the people they serve, as well as the Christian values she said the country was built on.

“We were built on God, and we have ventured away from that,” Camp said. “We need to get back there, but to get back there, people have to come out and vote.” <em>—⁠Alli Satterfield, The News Lab at Penn State </em>

{{<card hed="‘People are struggling’" timestamp="2026-11-03T08:11:00-05:00">}}
Samuel Copeland was one of the first voters to cast a ballot at the West Creek Hills Elementary School in Cumberland County’s East Pennsboro township after doors opened at 7 a.m.

This is a busy polling place, with voters from two precincts in the township casting ballots here — one is dominated by Republicans who went for President Donald Trump in the 2024 presidential election; the other is more evenly split and went for Kamala Harris in that election.

Copeland, who works at a local grocery store, has shifted his registration back and forth from Republican to Democrat over the years. He is currently a registered Democrat who volunteered for Janelle Stelson’s campaign. Stelson is running to unseat Republican U.S. Rep. Scott Perry in one of the most closely watched and hotly contested elections in Pennsylvania this year.

He said his biggest issue is a belief that politics needs “fresh, new faces,” people who are young, open-minded, and who will help the working class.

Affordability has been a problem for him, as he’s found himself spending more money on food and gas. Given his job — he said he&#39;s a floater who stocks shelves, pushes carts, and fills in wherever he is needed at the grocery store that day — he’s also seen and heard firsthand how people are feeling pinched paying for groceries.

“People are struggling,” he said. “They’re hurting.”

What brought him to the polls Tuesday was the Perry-Stelson race. He wants someone in Washington, D.C. who will stand up to President Donald Trump, whom he called “the worst president in history.”

“Poll numbers prove it, and everybody is upset about it. Even his supporters are turning on him because of what he’s doing,” said Copeland. “He said he was going to lower prices, but he’s done nothing he’s promised, and it’s making a lot of people angry.” <em>—Angela Couloumbis </em>

{{<card hed="‘Common sense issues’ in Cumberland County" timestamp="2026-11-03T07:49:00-05:00">}}
Voters were already starting to pull into the parking lot at West Creek Hills Elementary School in East Pennsboro, Cumberland County, shortly after 6:30 a.m.

This is a busy polling place, with voters from two precincts in the township casting ballots here — one is dominated by Republicans who went for President Donald Trump in the 2024 presidential election; the other is more evenly split and went for Kamala Harris in that election.

On the sidewalk outside, Scott Grob was setting up his table to promote Republican candidates, propping up his red pop-up tent and hammering lawn signs into the grass. Grob is a Cumberland County committeeman and registered Republican who said his signs reflect “common sense issues” that voters should care about.

The Democratic Party, he believes, has swung impossibly left and “once we get into communism, there is no way out.”

“My father was a Democrat. My grandfather was a Democrat,” said Grob, who was wearing a red hat with the phrase “Make Girls Sports Female Again.”

But before his dad died a few years back, Grob said, he believed the party had become unrecognizable.

“I know many young people don’t understand communism anymore,” he said. “But socialism has no place in the United States.” <em>—Angela Couloumbis </em>

{{<card hed="Election monitors in Pennsylvania" timestamp="2026-11-03T07:19:00-05:00">}}
The U.S. Department of Justice plans to send election monitors to polling places in Philadelphia, Allegheny, Lehigh, and Luzerne Counties.

Pennsylvania Deputy Secretary of the Commonwealth Jonathan Marks told counties in an email that the monitors “are not authorized by any specific state or federal statute” and “may not enter restricted areas, inspect voting equipment, or demand access to any records.”

A spokesperson for the Department of State clarified to Votebeat and Spotlight PA that “restricted areas” include the area starting at the 10-foot buffer zone from the entrance of a polling site, meaning, effectively, monitors cannot enter polling places.

More from our partners at <a href="https://www.votebeat.org/national/2026/11/03/election-day-2026-live-blog-midterms-trump/">Votebeat</a>:

“The Department of Justice had already announced that it would be sending around 1,000 election monitors to observe the voting today in all 50 states — the widest net the department has ever cast. But until this morning, we didn’t know the full list of exactly where in each state they would be. The department has now released that list; you can<a href="https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/doj-monitor-elections-all-50-states-first-time"> check it out here</a>.

As a reminder, Department of Justice monitors are a<a href="https://www.votebeat.org/national/2026/10/31/justice-department-election-monitors-explainer-2026-midterms/"> regular presence in every election</a>; if they do their job as they always have, they won’t interfere with voting. My colleagues will have more to say on the monitors later today.” <em>—Nathaniel Rakich, Votebeat </em>

{{<card hed="‘What do I need to bring with me to vote?’" timestamp="2026-11-03T05:00:00-05:00">}}
If this is your first time voting or your first time voting since changing addresses, you’ll need to bring proof of identification. This can include any government-issued ID such as a driver’s license or U.S. passport, a utility bill or bank statement that includes your name and address, or a military or student ID. <a href="https://www.vote.pa.gov/Register-to-Vote/Pages/Voter-ID-for-First-Time-Voters.aspx">See the full list of options</a>. <em>—Sarah Anne Hughes</em>

{{<card hed="How to report a problem while voting or get voting info" timestamp="2026-11-03T05:00:00-05:00">}}
If you need last-minute voting information, you can seek official answers from the Pennsylvania Department of State by calling 1-877-VOTESPA. The nonpartisan <a href="https://866ourvote.org/">Election Protection coalition</a> has its own hotline, as well: 866-OUR-VOTE.

If you need to lodge a complaint about something you experience while voting, you can call the state’s hotline (1-877-VOTESPA) or reach the department using this <a href="https://www.pavoterservices.pa.gov/Pages/ReportElectionComplaints.aspx">form</a>.

Still preparing to cast your ballot? Read Spotlight PA’s complete coverage at our<a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/elections/"> Election Center 2026 website</a>. <em>—Sarah Anne Hughes </em>

{{<card hed="What you need to know before heading to the polls" timestamp="2026-11-03T05:00:00-05:00">}}
For those of you heading out to the polls, here’s what you need to know:

- Polls are open from 7 a.m. to 8 p.m. Double-check your polling place <a href="https://www.pavoterservices.pa.gov/Pages/PollingPlaceInfo.aspx">here</a>.

- In addition to nonpartisan poll workers, you may encounter partisan poll watchers who are sanctioned to observe polling places. <a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2024/03/pennsylvania-poll-watcher-election-2024-donald-trump-explainer/">Here’s what they can — and can’t — do</a>.

- Have a completed mail ballot? You can still drop it off today by 8 p.m. Some satellite offices and drop boxes will close before then, <a href="https://www.pa.gov/en/agencies/vote/voter-support/mail-in-and-absentee-ballot/return-ballot.html">so make sure to look up your local details</a>. <strong>Do not put it in the mail. It will not arrive by the deadline. </strong>

- Have a mail ballot but want to vote in person? Bring all of the materials with you to your polling place.

- Requested a mail ballot but it never came? You can still vote using a<a href="https://www.pa.gov/en/agencies/vote/voter-support/provisional-ballot.html"> provisional ballot</a>.

- If you are facing a last-minute problem and can’t make it to the polls, you may qualify for an emergency absentee ballot. <a href="https://www.pa.gov/en/agencies/vote/voter-support/mail-in-and-absentee-ballot/emergency-absentee-ballot.html">Read about those here</a>.

Election officials can only begin processing mail and absentee ballots this morning,<a href="https://www.spotlightpa.org/news/2024/10/pennsylvania-election-mail-ballots-precanvassing-101/"> which means it may take days to report the full unofficial results</a> in some competitive races. <em>—Sarah Anne Hughes </em>
