{{<card hed="Minor issues in Cambria County" timestamp="2026-11-03T11:26:00-05:00">}}
This morning, reports were circulating online of what sounded, to me, like pollbook issues in Cambria County. You may recall that Cambria County had<a href="https://www.wtae.com/article/pennsylvania-voting-machine-error-cambria-county/62816240"> a significant issue in 2024</a> with tabulators being unable to scan ballots.

Shortly after polls opened this morning, conservative activist Scott Presler posted on X that a polling place in Johnstown was “turning people away” as a result of voters’ names being missing, presumably from pollbooks.

I asked the county what was happening, and they sent me<a href="https://www.documentcloud.org/documents/28864991-minor-issues-corrected-at-polling-places/"> this statement</a>. TL;DR: One precinct did not receive a complete pollbook, but a replacement was printed and sent to the polling place. The issue did not prevent any voter from casting a ballot.

Minor issues like this sometimes happen in elections, which is why there are contingency plans like provisional ballots to ensure voters can still vote.—<em>Carter Walker, Votebeat </em>

{{<card hed="Different choices in the Lehigh Valley" timestamp="2026-11-03T10:52:00-05:00">}}
At Easton City Hall, in the city’s Second Ward, a line of voters snaked across the lobby.

Logan Cole, 25, said he voted “Democrat down the board.”

“I can’t say I’m very informed when it comes to midterms specifically, but I feel more strongly about one side over the other,” he told Spotlight PA.

A chef who has lived on his own the past few years, Cole said the economy, healthcare, and education are important issues to him.

“The economy is a large factor, \[but\] just the nonsense of it all,” he said, pointing to Republican majorities at the federal level.

“Priorities are completely out of whack,” he said. “This isn’t an America-first government. It’s also an uneducated government.”

At Bethlehem City Hall, in the city’s Sixth Ward, Mike Garcia, 86, said he’s hopeful for change.

“I vote for the individual,” Garcia, a registered Republican, told Spotlight PA. “I don’t go by party.”

However, he voted with his party in both the 7th Congressional District and gubernatorial races.

Republican incumbent U.S. Rep. Ryan Mackenzie is “very dedicated to the county,” Garcia said, adding that he “cares about the people.”

Garcia said he’s “displeased” with Gov. Josh Shapiro.

Stacy Garrity “is a good treasurer, a good person,” Garcia said. “I think she can make good changes.”

Adriana Trumbower, a 22-year-old house cleaner who is registered as an independent, told Spotlight PA it’s her first time voting in a midterm election, adding “it’s really important for people to vote, and I think it’s important we start going in another direction as a country.”

She cast her ballot for Democrats, including Gov. Josh Shapiro and 7th Congressional District candidate Bob Brooks.

“I really love Shapiro,” Trumbower said, highlighting his support for women’s reproductive rights.

“I voted for Bob Brooks because I really don’t like Ryan Mackenzie,” she said. She listed several issues she disagrees with Mackenzie on, including his vote for President Donald Trump’s “One Big Beautiful Bill,” his position on abortion, and that he “takes money from corporations.” <em>—Molly Bilinski </em>

{{<card hed="Lehigh County voters tired of polarization" image="cas/cssj-nbmy-tr50-scej.jpeg" image-credit="Jaxon White / Spotlight PA" image-description="Lehigh County voters Ryan Durkin (left) and Jevata Crawford" timestamp="2026-11-03T10:41:00-05:00">}}
Jevata Crawford, 53, and Ryan Durkin, 55, had never met before the morning of Election Day. But the pair struck up a conversation while they waited in line to cast their ballots at St. Anne’s Episcopal Church in Lower Macungie Township, Lehigh County.

Despite Crawford being a Democrat and Durkin being an independent, they told Spotlight PA they found themselves agreeing on a lot — especially that the polarization and volume of political advertising in this year’s contested races was out of hand.

It just seemed “wasteful” to Crawford, who noted that “finances are tight for so many people.”

Their area is a petri dish of competitive and expensive races this year, from U.S. Congress to state Senate to state House.

Affordability and the rising costs of goods, Crawford said, were the issues top of her mind when she filled out her ballot for Democratic candidates.

The personal attacks, like those launched between Republican U.S. Rep. Ryan Mackenzie and Democrat Bob Brooks in the 7th Congressional District, also upset Crawford and Durkin. (One example Crawford cited was Mackenzie criticizing Brooks over a lawsuit sparked by the Democrat being indebted to his former mother-in-law.)

That race is one of four congressional seats that Democrats aim to flip this year as the party hopes to seize a majority in the U.S. House of Representatives.

Crawford and Durkin said they’re tired of polarization making it difficult to have conversations with friends and family members about politics.

Durkin, a self-identified split-ticket voter, said the economy and personal finances are what he and the vast majority of voters care most about. This year, these issues are even more prominent because of general spikes in costs and the war in Iran driving up oil prices, he said.

Durkin said he’s also frustrated with congressional Republicans who have “abrogated” their responsibilities to allow President Donald Trump to take more power.

Speaking about Trump, Crawford and Durkin — in unison — called the president an “opportunist.”

As Durkin and Crawford headed to their cars parked in the church’s lot, they waved and said they looked forward to seeing each other at their local grocery store. <em>—Jaxon White </em>

{{<card hed="Healthcare motivates a retired physician" image="cas/tnce-bf18-yqc7-gqxf.jpeg" image-credit="Angela Couloumbis / Spotlight PA" image-description="Gunhilde Beck is a retired physician and registered Democrat living in a very red area: Dauphin Borough." timestamp="2026-11-03T10:12:00-05:00">}}
Gunhilde Beck is a retired physician and registered Democrat living in a very red area: Dauphin Borough in central Pennsylvania’s Dauphin County.

Republican voters here outnumber Democrats, and the borough has gone for President Donald Trump in the last three presidential elections. So Beck is somewhat of a rarity here.

But on Tuesday, she got up before dawn and set up a lawn chair outside the borough’s polling station at the municipal building. She planted signs for Democrats, including Gov. Josh Shapiro and congressional hopeful Janelle Stelson, who is challenging U.S. Rep. Scott Perry. She made the sign for Democrat Kathryn Waters, who is trying to unseat GOP state Rep. Joe Kerwin in what is widely considered a very uphill battle.

She made the sign, she said, because Waters’ campaign is not flush with enough cash for campaign merchandise.

Beck, who describes herself as an “environmentalist, pro-science, and anti-corruption,” is originally from Germany but has lived in the borough for five decades. She was never political, she said, until Trump won in 2016. And in this year’s election, she decided to volunteer for the Democratic Party for the first time, going door-to-door canvassing.

One of her biggest issues is healthcare.

“The money goes to insurance companies and to hospitals and to providers; it doesn’t go to a patient,” she said. “But now we are blaming the poor people. And now we are taking their Medicaid away, and they are ending up in the emergency rooms, and we are paying more for them.”

“And vaccinations,” she said. “My mind explodes thinking about RFK being our health secretary during a measles epidemic. I’ve never seen a measles case in my life. We are taking all the science away. Corruption at every level.”

Beck said she thinks it’s important for voters to understand how important state elections for governor and the legislature are. She believes the divided General Assembly — the House is controlled by Democrats, the Senate by Republicans — has amounted to gridlock that has prevented good policies, including increasing the minimum wage, from being approved.
